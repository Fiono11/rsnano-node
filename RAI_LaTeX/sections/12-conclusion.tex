\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
RAI's checkpoints grow with the unresolved forks they keep, and locks, late supports, and overlap witnesses must be kept while a lock they can release survives; we give no garbage-collection policy beyond the prototype's four-epoch cap. The committee model is authenticated and equal-weight with an externally kept fault bound. With an equivocating validator and 10\% forks the prototype settles across six regions at 1{,}000 blocks/s but not at 1{,}500; the committee change and the restarts were measured on one host only; each cell ran three times; and process kills test crash consistency, not power loss. The models cover at most two positions and four epochs and check the recipe only by simulation and to 17 steps.

Within these limits RAI does what it set out to do. It keeps account-local payments safe across committee changes without stopping: the next epoch votes while the previous one closes, immutable reports preserve whatever the old epoch could still confirm, and four overlap rules keep the two epochs from contradicting each other. It keeps at most one side of every contested position, identically everywhere, and finalizes that side within two epochs of normal operation, so the owner always gets the account back. Across six regions, against a stop-and-drain transition built from the same code, it keeps confirming through every boundary, with a 95th percentile ten to sixteen times lower. Against a system without epochs it finalizes the forks that system leaves open, with a lower median and a lower 95th percentile in most cases, on a normal path that needs fewer votes, and it confirms almost twice as many blocks per second when forks are frequent. In return it can change its validators, and its protocol: every validator agrees on the epoch boundaries and the checkpoint that closes each epoch, so a protocol change can take effect at an agreed epoch, where Nano appends an epoch block to every account.
