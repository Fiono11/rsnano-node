\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\input{sections/eval-figures}
The evaluation asks three questions. Does RAI resolve the forks Nano leaves open, and what does that cost in confirmation rate and latency? Does it keep confirming payments while an epoch closes, where a stop-and-drain transition stops, also when a validator is Byzantine? Does a committee change disturb it?

\para{Prototype.}
We implemented RAI in RsNano~\cite{rsnano}, a Rust implementation of the Nano node~\cite{nano}. Account elections follow Section~\ref{sec:epoch}, with late notarization, the early-vote rule, and the prefix-wide overlap rule; checkpoints carry origin-stamped locks and are built by the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:close}. Epochs end at fixed times $T_0+kD$ on every validator's local clock. The content of every vote, the first votes behind every final vote, and every frozen report, decided checkpoint, and overlap certificate are synced to disk before a signature that depends on them leaves the process, so a restarted validator refuses whatever its earlier signatures forbid, reports what it notarized, and signs its recorded votes again to serve them as evidence. Set reconstruction uses rateless invertible Bloom lookup tables~\cite{rateless}. Agreement is one leader-based election per handover in the style of Kudzu~\cite{kudzu}: a validator votes for a proposal only after reconstructing its reports, running the recipe, and matching the state digest; leaders rotate, and a round whose leader stays silent times out after 2\,s. A validator does not repeat a request for votes before its reply could arrive: the floor is set to the longest measured round trip. An instance that has no first vote from this validator, in an epoch whose predecessor is not installed, does not ask at all, since the others hold back too. The supplement lists where the prototype departs from the protocol.

\para{What we compare.}
\emph{RAI} is the protocol of this paper with $f=p=1$ on six members: a certificate needs four of six, and the fast path five settled first votes. \emph{Pause} is the same code with one switch flipped: no instance of epoch $e+1$ starts before $S_e$ is decided locally. It is the stop-and-drain transition RAI set out to avoid, with reports, recipe, agreement, and installation unchanged. \emph{Nano} is the RsNano \texttt{develop} node (commit \texttt{ff2b32710}) with one optimization of RAI's prototype ported to it, checking a fork block's signature once: a block is confirmed once final votes reach 67\% of the online weight, five of six here, after a round of non-final votes. It has no epochs and no committee, and since final votes cannot be withdrawn, a fork whose votes split leaves neither side able to confirm. RAI and Nano both tolerate one Byzantine validator, but RAI's normal path needs fewer votes.

\para{Setup.}
Six equal-weight validators run on separate Google Cloud VMs (n2-standard-4, four vCPUs), one each in Warsaw, Iowa, Montr\'eal, Las Vegas, Taiwan, and Melbourne, with round trips from about 30 to over 300\,ms. A load generator on another VM in Warsaw publishes 45{,}000 blocks at 2{,}000 blocks/s, each on the frontier of a randomly chosen account, with the same random choices for the three systems. Setup funds 2{,}000 accounts, enough that publishing holds the offered rate from the first block; the funded ledgers are saved once per setup and restored before each run. An account publishes its next block only once its last one is confirmed, so a system that confirms slowly is offered less: the client published under 95\% of the offered non-fork rate in 20 of pause's 36 runs, down to half of it, and in 4 of RAI's and 2 of Nano's, down to 75 and 85\%. In the fork scenarios it publishes a conflicting sibling for 5\% or 10\% of the blocks, each sibling to half of the validators, and never builds on a forked position, so what is left open measures what the validators resolve on their own. In the Byzantine scenarios one validator runs a modified node: it votes for both blocks of every fork, signs two versions of its report and sends each to half of the validators, and leads the first round of every close with two different proposals. Nano's Byzantine validator votes for both blocks of every fork, the one attack Nano has a phase for. RAI and pause use 8\,s epochs, so a close starts about every 8\,s during the load. The \emph{confirmation rate} counts the non-fork blocks the client sees confirmed per second over the whole run, so with 10\% forks it cannot exceed 1{,}800. \emph{Latency} runs from a block's first publication to its confirmation at the Warsaw validator and counts non-fork blocks only. After the load, the validators keep running until no block is open on any of them, or until none has become final for 30\,s or the harness's time limit is reached. Each cell ran three times; we report the median and the range. A run passes when every validator holds the same blocks and the decided values agree and, for RAI and pause, every block is final everywhere.

\para{Latency and confirmation rate (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-matrix}).}
All 108 runs passed. Without forks RAI has a median of 390\,ms and a 95th percentile of 483\,ms against Nano's 535 and 592\,ms, and the two confirm about the same (1{,}954 and 1{,}928 blocks/s). Nano needs five of six votes in both of its rounds, so it waits for a distant validator twice; RAI's normal path waits for four, and 14 to 69\% of its finalizations, depending on the validator and run, take the one-round fast path. With 5\% and 10\% forks RAI's median stays at 385 and 400\,ms and its 95th percentile at 527 and 571\,ms, at 1{,}858 and 1{,}750 blocks/s. Nano keeps its median (529 and 533\,ms) and has a 95th percentile of 612 and 828\,ms, but with 10\% forks its 99th percentile is 14 to 19\,s and it confirms 946 blocks/s, since the run lasts until its last non-fork block is confirmed. With a Byzantine validator RAI's median is 391 to 400\,ms and its 95th percentile 508 to 721\,ms (994\,ms in one run), at 1{,}747 to 1{,}945 blocks/s; Nano's are 529 to 535\,ms and 595 to 1{,}044\,ms, at 740 to 1{,}912 blocks/s. Pause is behind both: over its runs the median is 0.5 to 6.5\,s and the 95th percentile 4.7 to 23\,s. Cell by cell its median is 1.3 to 12 times RAI's and its 95th percentile 9 to 18 times, and RAI confirms up to 2.2 times as many blocks per second (the two tie only without forks and without a Byzantine validator).

\para{RAI resolves forks (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-forks}).}
With forks, RAI finalized every open block within 15 to 21\,s of the end of the load in all six runs without a Byzantine validator, and within 22 to 40\,s with one. Pause took 14 to 21\,s and 22 to 43\,s. In all of these runs every validator cemented the same state. RAI's recipe counts settled first votes only, so a fork whose first votes all came before the previous checkpoint was installed waits one more epoch. Nano did not finalize them all. When the harness stopped waiting it had 3 to 915 blocks open on a validator without a Byzantine validator and 13 to 1{,}303 with one. Across all 24 of its runs with forks, committee change included, its validators held the same blocks, but in 14 of them had cemented different numbers of them.

\para{RAI keeps confirming while an epoch closes (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-timeline}).}
Pause holds new blocks while an epoch closes and then confirms them in a burst. With 10\% forks and a Byzantine validator it had 14 to 20 seconds without a single confirmation, up to 13 in a row, and averaged 362 to 659 blocks/s while an epoch closed. RAI, on the same load, had no such second in two runs and eight, up to four in a row, in the third, and averaged 1{,}626 to 1{,}738 blocks/s while an epoch closed. Without a Byzantine validator RAI had no such second across nine runs and averaged 1{,}724 to 1{,}977 blocks/s during closes; pause had 5 to 8 and averaged 143 to 971. RAI's closes are not short: with 10\% forks and a Byzantine validator the longest of each run takes 5.1 to 13.9\,s, up to longer than an epoch, since a close must wait for the reports of all five honest validators (pause: 16 to 25\,s). But no block waits for them unless the fork rules hold it. Each validator finds at least 12{,}900 of the 45{,}000 blocks eligible for the overlap rule.

\para{A committee change (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-rotation}).}
A seventh validator in Toronto starts outside the committee with a small balance, and Montr\'eal moves its whole balance to an account delegating to it during epoch~1, so the committee derived from that epoch, the one epoch~3 runs, has Toronto in Montr\'eal's seat, about 14\,s into the load. Without a Byzantine validator RAI's median is 386 to 400\,ms and its 95th percentile 560 to 700\,ms (2.0 and 2.3\,s in two runs), at 1{,}735 to 1{,}945 blocks/s, close to the cells without the change; its slowest closes take 3.9 to 11.5\,s, and it finalizes every open block within 25\,s of the end of the load. With a Byzantine validator that keeps its seat, the median is 408 to 435\,ms and the 95th percentile 548 to 786\,ms (1.2 and 1.6\,s in two runs) at 1{,}741 to 1{,}946 blocks/s, and every block becomes final within 34\,s. Closes are slowest with forks, a Byzantine validator, and the change: the slowest close of each run takes 6.3 to 14.8\,s, against 8\,s epochs. Pause's median is 0.5 to 3.4\,s and its 95th percentile 4.7 to 30\,s, 6.7 to 38 times RAI's. Nano has no committee; moving the balance only changes its weights. Every member holds an equal share and the standby a sixty-fourth of one, so its threshold still takes five of six votes: its median is 530 to 537\,ms, as in its cells without the change, and it confirms 410 to 1{,}081 blocks/s with 10\% forks.

\para{Restarts and handoff.}
A second harness, on the same host, kills validators mid-load or as a close starts, restarts them on their data, and checks every logged signature for conflicts. Restarted validators rejoined by checkpoint catch-up, and a departing member killed when its last close starts and kept down 30\,s left every checkpoint decided identically. No run produced a conflicting signature. Signing recorded votes again and restoring notarizations after a restart are covered by unit tests and five further restart runs on this host (no conflicting signature, every fork resolved, every notarization restored), not by the runs above; on this host, writing those notarizations changed neither confirmation rate nor latency beyond the spread between runs. Synced signing costs about 130\,ms at the median and nothing in confirmation rate. The supplement reports these runs.

\para{Threats to validity.}
RAI's normal path needs four votes and Nano's five, so part of RAI's lower latency comes from the smaller quorum, not from the protocol's other rules. Latency is measured at one validator, beside the load generator; a client elsewhere would see other numbers. It covers only the blocks the client published, and pause, which confirms slowly, was offered less than the full rate, so its latency is understated. With an earlier build a validator could fall up to 158\,s behind after the burst of votes at an epoch switch, because its voter held the elections lock for a whole scan and starved vote processing; every RAI and pause run here uses the build that holds the lock per 64 elections, and no RAI validator entered an epoch more than 3.8\,s after the median one. Eleven runs in which the client stopped publishing for 4 to 5\,s, waiting on a write to one validator, were repeated with the same seed and a client that writes to each validator separately; the supplement lists them. Nano's cells without the committee change come from an earlier matrix with the same Nano node. Restarts were not run across regions, and the six-region runs did not audit signatures for conflicts. Three runs per cell bound the spread only roughly, and process kills test crash consistency, not power loss.
