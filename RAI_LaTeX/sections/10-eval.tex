\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\input{sections/eval-figures}
The evaluation asks three questions. Does RAI resolve the forks Nano leaves open, and what does that cost in confirmation rate and latency? Does it keep confirming payments while an epoch closes, where a stop-and-drain transition stops, also when a validator is Byzantine? Does a committee change disturb it?

\para{Prototype.}
We implemented RAI in RsNano~\cite{rsnano}, a Rust implementation of the Nano node~\cite{nano}. Account elections follow Section~\ref{sec:epoch}, with late notarization, the early-vote rule, and the prefix-wide overlap rule; checkpoints carry origin-stamped locks and are built by the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:close}. Epochs end at fixed times $T_0+kD$ on every validator's local clock. The content of every vote, the first votes behind every final vote, and every frozen report, decided checkpoint, and overlap certificate are synced to disk before a signature that depends on them leaves the process, so a restarted validator refuses whatever its earlier signatures forbid, reports what it notarized, and signs its recorded votes again to serve them as evidence. Set reconstruction uses rateless invertible Bloom lookup tables~\cite{rateless}. Agreement is one leader-based election per handover in the style of Kudzu~\cite{kudzu}: a validator votes for a proposal only after reconstructing its reports, running the recipe, and matching the state digest; leaders rotate, and a round whose leader stays silent times out after 2\,s. A validator does not repeat a request for votes before its reply could arrive: the floor is set to the longest measured round trip. An instance that has no first vote from this validator, in an epoch whose predecessor is not installed, does not ask at all, since the others hold back too. The supplement lists where the prototype departs from the protocol.

\para{What we compare.}
\emph{RAI} is the protocol of this paper with $f=p=1$ on six members: a certificate needs four of six, and the fast path five settled first votes. \emph{Pause} is the same code with one switch flipped: no instance of epoch $e+1$ starts before $S_e$ is decided locally. It is the stop-and-drain transition RAI set out to avoid, with reports, recipe, agreement, and installation unchanged. \emph{Nano} is the RsNano \texttt{develop} node (commit \texttt{ff2b32710}) with one optimization of RAI's prototype ported to it, checking a fork block's signature once: a block is confirmed once final votes reach 67\% of the online weight, five of six here, after a round of non-final votes. It has no epochs and no committee, and since final votes cannot be withdrawn, a fork whose votes split leaves neither side able to confirm. RAI and Nano both tolerate one Byzantine validator, but RAI's normal path needs fewer votes.

\para{Setup.}
Six equal-weight validators run on separate Google Cloud VMs (n2-standard-4, four vCPUs), one each in Warsaw, Iowa, Montr\'eal, Las Vegas, Taiwan, and Melbourne, with round trips from about 30 to over 300\,ms. A load generator on another VM in Warsaw publishes 45{,}000 blocks at 2{,}000 blocks/s, each on the frontier of a randomly chosen account, with the same random choices for the three systems. Setup funds 2{,}000 accounts, enough that publishing holds the offered rate from the first block; the funded ledgers are saved once per setup and restored before each run. In the fork scenarios it publishes a conflicting sibling for 5\% or 10\% of the blocks, each sibling to half of the validators, and never builds on a forked position, so what is left open measures what the validators resolve on their own. In the Byzantine scenarios one validator runs a modified node: it votes for both blocks of every fork, signs two versions of its report and sends each to half of the validators, and leads the first round of every close with two different proposals. Nano's Byzantine validator votes for both blocks of every fork, the one attack Nano has a phase for. RAI and pause use 8\,s epochs, so a close starts about every 8\,s during the load. The \emph{confirmation rate} counts the non-fork blocks the client sees confirmed per second over the whole run, so with 10\% forks it cannot exceed 1{,}800. \emph{Latency} runs from a block's first publication to its confirmation at the Warsaw validator and counts non-fork blocks only. After the load, the validators keep running until no block is open on any of them, or until none has become final for 30\,s or the harness's time limit is reached. Each cell ran three times; we report the median and the range. A run passes when every validator holds the same blocks and the decided values agree and, for RAI and pause, every block is final everywhere.

\para{Latency and confirmation rate (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-matrix}).}
All 108 runs passed. Without forks RAI has a median of 384\,ms and a 95th percentile of 522\,ms against Nano's 535 and 592\,ms, and the two confirm about the same (1{,}945 and 1{,}928 blocks/s). Nano needs five of six votes in both of its rounds, so it waits for a distant validator twice; RAI's normal path waits for four, and 16 to 69\% of its finalizations, depending on the validator and run, take the one-round fast path. With 5\% and 10\% forks RAI's median stays at 385 and 392\,ms and its 95th percentile at 540 and 627\,ms, at 1{,}831 and 1{,}740 blocks/s. Nano keeps its median (529 and 533\,ms) and has a 95th percentile of 612 and 828\,ms, but with 10\% forks its 99th percentile is 14 to 19\,s and it confirms 946 blocks/s, since the run lasts until its last non-fork block is confirmed. With a Byzantine validator RAI's median is 388 to 412\,ms and its 95th percentile 494 to 954\,ms (up to 2.0\,s in one run), at 1{,}749 to 1{,}928 blocks/s; Nano's are 529 to 535\,ms and 595 to 1{,}044\,ms, at 740 to 1{,}912 blocks/s. Pause is far behind both: its median is 0.6 to 15\,s and its 95th percentile 5.3 to 48\,s, ten to fifty times RAI's, and RAI confirms up to 3.5 times as many blocks per second (the two tie only with 5\% forks and no Byzantine validator).

\para{RAI resolves forks (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-forks}).}
With forks, RAI finalized every open block within 15 to 16\,s of the end of the load in five of the six runs without a Byzantine validator, and in 149\,s in the sixth, where one validator fell behind; with one it took 21 to 38\,s. Pause took 16 to 28\,s and 27 to 77\,s. In all of these runs every validator cemented the same state. RAI's recipe counts settled first votes only, so a fork whose first votes all came before the previous checkpoint was installed waits one more epoch. Nano did not finalize them all. When the harness stopped waiting it had 3 to 915 blocks open on a validator without a Byzantine validator and 13 to 1{,}303 with one. Across all 24 of its runs with forks, committee change included, its validators held the same blocks, but in 18 of them had cemented different numbers of them.

\para{RAI keeps confirming while an epoch closes (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-timeline}).}
Pause holds new blocks while an epoch closes and then confirms them in a burst. With 10\% forks and a Byzantine validator it had 18 to 25 seconds without a single confirmation, up to 17 in a row, and averaged 30 to 358 blocks/s while an epoch closed. RAI, on the same load, had no such second in two runs and five in a row in the third, and averaged 1{,}326 to 1{,}750 blocks/s while an epoch closed. Without a Byzantine validator RAI had one such second across nine runs and averaged 1{,}653 to 1{,}980 blocks/s during closes; pause had 5 to 7 and averaged 111 to 266. RAI's closes are not short: with 10\% forks and a Byzantine validator the longest of each run takes 8.1 to 10.7\,s, longer than an epoch, since a close must wait for the reports of all five honest validators (pause: 26 to 38\,s). But no block waits for them unless the fork rules hold it. Each validator finds at least 12{,}800 of the 45{,}000 blocks eligible for the overlap rule.

\para{A committee change (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-rotation}).}
A seventh validator in Toronto starts outside the committee with a small balance, and Montr\'eal moves its whole balance to an account delegating to it during epoch~1, so the committee derived from that epoch, the one epoch~3 runs, has Toronto in Montr\'eal's seat, about 14\,s into the load. Without a Byzantine validator RAI's median is 378 to 386\,ms and its 95th percentile 532 to 717\,ms, at 1{,}744 to 1{,}945 blocks/s, as without the change; its slowest closes take 3.9 to 6.6\,s, and it finalizes every open block within 26\,s of the end of the load. With a Byzantine validator that keeps its seat, the median is 396 to 418\,ms and the 95th percentile 542 to 1{,}011\,ms at 1{,}654 to 1{,}945 blocks/s, and every block becomes final within 87\,s. The hardest cell, 10\% forks with a Byzantine validator and the change, is the one where closes fall behind: the slowest close of each run takes 13 to 53\,s. Pause's median is 1.1 to 5.3\,s and its 95th percentile 6.0 to 31\,s. Nano has no committee; moving the balance only changes its weights, and here its threshold can be met by four votes when the genesis account's larger balance is among them, so its median of 404 to 417\,ms does not compare with its five-vote cells. It still confirms 474 to 562 blocks/s with 10\% forks.

\para{Restarts and handoff.}
A second harness, on the same host, kills validators mid-load or as a close starts, restarts them on their data, and checks every logged signature for conflicts. Restarted validators rejoined by checkpoint catch-up, and a departing member killed when its last close starts and kept down 30\,s left every checkpoint decided identically. No run produced a conflicting signature. Signing recorded votes again and restoring notarizations after a restart are covered by unit tests and five further restart runs on this host (no conflicting signature, every fork resolved, every notarization restored), not by the runs above; on this host, writing those notarizations changed neither confirmation rate nor latency beyond the spread between runs. Synced signing costs about 130\,ms at the median and nothing in confirmation rate. The supplement reports these runs.

\para{Threats to validity.}
RAI's normal path needs four votes and Nano's five, so part of RAI's lower latency comes from the smaller quorum, not from the protocol's other rules. Latency is measured at one validator, beside the load generator; a client elsewhere would see other numbers. In some runs one validator falls far behind, Toronto in most of them, asks for what it lacks, and slows the closes that wait for its report: 6 of the 22 RAI attempts with the committee change, counting those we repeated, and one with 10\% forks and no Byzantine validator or change, where a close took 54\,s and the last block 149\,s. Blocks stay safe and become final, but we did not find what starts it, and the medians hide it: read the ranges. The supplement lists the runs we repeated: three that a harness limit stopped while blocks were still becoming final and one in which a validator stayed behind, all repeated with the same seed, and two lost to a preempted VM. Six RAI and pause runs, in the committee-change cells with forks and a Byzantine validator, used a build that also caps a validator's requests. Restarts were not run across regions, and the six-region runs did not audit signatures for conflicts. Three runs per cell bound the spread only roughly, and process kills test crash consistency, not power loss.
