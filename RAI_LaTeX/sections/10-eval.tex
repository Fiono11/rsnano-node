\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\input{sections/eval-figures}
The evaluation asks three questions. Does RAI keep confirming payments while an epoch closes, where a stop-and-drain transition stops? What does it cost in confirmation rate and latency against Nano, which has no epochs at all? And does it resolve the forks that Nano leaves open?

\para{Prototype.}
We implemented RAI in RsNano~\cite{rsnano}, a Rust implementation of the Nano node~\cite{nano}. Account elections follow Section~\ref{sec:epoch} with the thresholds of Table~\ref{tab:thresholds} ($f=p=1$, $N=6$), late notarization, the early-vote rule, and the prefix-wide overlap rule; checkpoints carry origin-stamped locks and are built by the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:close}. Epochs end at fixed times $T_0+kD$ on every validator's local clock. Every vote, frozen report, decided checkpoint, and overlap certificate is synced to disk before a signature that depends on it leaves the process, so a restarted validator refuses whatever its earlier signatures forbid. Set reconstruction uses rateless invertible Bloom lookup tables~\cite{rateless}. Agreement is one leader-based election per handover in the style of Kudzu~\cite{kudzu}: a validator votes for a proposal only after reconstructing its reports, running the recipe, and matching the state digest; leaders rotate, and a round whose leader is silent times out after 2\,s. The supplement lists the prototype's departures from the protocol and the defects the measurements found.

\para{What we compare.}
\emph{RAI} is the protocol of this paper. \emph{Pause} is the same code with one switch flipped: no instance of epoch $e+1$ starts before $S_e$ is decided locally. It is the stop-and-drain transition RAI set out to avoid, with reports, recipe, agreement, and installation unchanged. \emph{Nano} is the unmodified RsNano \texttt{develop} node (commit \texttt{ff2b32710}): a block is confirmed once final votes reach 67\% of the online weight, after a round of non-final votes. It has no epochs and no committee, and since final votes cannot be withdrawn, a fork whose votes split leaves neither side able to confirm.

\para{Setup.}
Six equal-weight validators run as separate processes on one MacBook Air (Apple M1, eight cores, 16\,GiB) over loopback. A load generator funds 45{,}000 accounts and then publishes 45{,}000 blocks at 2{,}000 blocks/s, for 22.5\,s, each on the frontier of a randomly chosen account, with the same random choices for the three systems. In the fork scenarios it publishes a conflicting sibling for 5\% or 10\% of the blocks, each sibling to half of the validators, and never builds on a forked position, so what is left open measures what the validators resolve on their own. In the Byzantine scenario one validator leads the first round of every close and stays silent in it. RAI and pause use 8\,s epochs, so two closes, and in most runs a third, start while the load runs. The \emph{confirmation rate} counts the non-fork blocks the client sees confirmed in each second of the load. \emph{Latency} runs from a block's first publication to its confirmation at one validator and counts non-fork blocks only. After the load, the validators keep running until no block is open on any of them, or until none has become final for 45\,s. Each scenario ran once. At 2{,}000 blocks/s the host is close to saturation: the six validators compete for its eight cores, and in RAI an epoch close competes with voting.

\para{RAI keeps confirming while an epoch closes (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-timeline}).}
Pause confirms nothing between the moment a validator leaves an epoch and the moment the checkpoint is decided, then confirms the held blocks in a burst. With 10\% forks it had six seconds without a single confirmation and averaged 417 non-fork blocks/s while an epoch closed; with a Byzantine leader, which makes every close wait for the 2\,s round timeout, it had nine such seconds and confirmed nothing at all during closes. RAI never had a second without confirmations: while an epoch closed it confirmed 1{,}484 to 1{,}808 non-fork blocks/s across the four scenarios, against 0 to 915 for pause. RAI's closes take longer than pause's, 1.4 to 12.0\,s against 0.9 to 7.2\,s while the load runs, because its validators keep voting meanwhile, but no block waits for them: blocks published during a close have a 95th percentile of 0.36 to 3.9\,s under RAI and 2.8 to 10.6\,s under pause.

\para{Confirmation rate (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-rate}).}
Over the load, RAI confirms 1{,}582 to 1{,}936 non-fork blocks/s. Pause confirms as much without forks or with 5\% forks, where its bursts make up for its stops, but 25\% less with 10\% forks and 26\% less with a Byzantine leader, where its stops are longest; its lowest second is zero in every scenario, RAI's 193 to 1{,}599. Nano, which has no closes, confirms the most in every scenario, 1{,}716 to 1{,}990 blocks/s, 3 to 13\% more than RAI, and its lowest second is 915 to 1{,}764.

\para{Latency (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-latency}).}
Without forks RAI has the lowest median, 138\,ms against Nano's 195\,ms: an uncontested block finalizes on one round of settled first votes, where Nano always needs a round of final votes. With forks its median rises to 312 to 693\,ms, above Nano's 189 to 211\,ms. RAI's 95th percentile is 0.34 to 3.5\,s; pause's is 2.0 to 6.4 times higher, 2.1 to 10.2\,s, because every block published during a close waits for the checkpoint. Nano's 95th percentile, 226 to 962\,ms, is the lowest in every scenario, but with 10\% forks its tail reaches further: its 99th percentile is 13.5\,s against RAI's 4.1\,s, the backlog it confirms in a trickle after the load.

\para{RAI resolves forks (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-forks}).}
Shortly after the load with 10\% forks ended, up to 3{,}053 blocks were open under RAI and 1{,}386 under pause, forked positions and the blocks behind them; both finalized all of them within 16\,s of the end of the load, and every validator cemented the same state. Nano still had 2{,}351 open 14\,s after the load and stopped making progress with 6 open on every validator. With 5\% forks RAI and pause finalized every open block within 11\,s of the end of the load, and within 23\,s with a Byzantine leader, while Nano left 2{,}352 and 446 open.

\para{Restarts, committee changes, and handoff.}
A second harness kills validators mid-load or as a close starts, restarts them on their data, and checks every logged signature for conflicts. Restarted validators rejoined by checkpoint catch-up. With eight validators of which six form the committee, replacing two members, replacing them with a Byzantine member in every committee, and replacing every correct member of a four-member committee all settled with identical checkpoints everywhere, and a departing member killed when its last close starts and kept down 30\,s left every checkpoint decided identically. No run produced a conflicting signature. Synced signing costs about 130\,ms at the median and nothing in goodput. The supplement reports these runs. A committee change in every epoch under load is beyond this host: with eight node processes, rotating runs completed at 500 blocks/s, but at 1{,}000 blocks/s and above a rotated-in validator fell behind its peers in most runs of both RAI and pause.

\para{Threats to validity.}
All validators share one host and a loopback network, so delays are dominated by CPU contention. At 2{,}000 blocks/s an epoch close competes with voting for the same cores, which is where RAI's latency tail comes from, and RAI does more work per block than pause (late notarizations and overlap evidence), so its confirmation rate may stay below Nano's on separate machines too. The load spans two to three closes. In longer runs with 10\% forks at this rate and 12 or 16\,s epochs, each close took longer than an epoch on this host and the next one started later, so for that scenario the results are not a steady state. Each scenario ran once, so the numbers are indicative, not statistical. The Byzantine validator votes at random apart from leading every close, and process kills test crash consistency, not power loss.
