\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\input{sections/eval-figures}
The evaluation asks four questions. At the same quorum as Nano, what does RAI cost in confirmation rate and latency, and does it resolve the forks Nano leaves open? Does it keep confirming payments while an epoch closes, where a stop-and-drain transition stops, also when a validator is Byzantine? Does a committee change disturb it? And what does the fast path buy?

\para{Prototype.}
We implemented RAI in RsNano~\cite{rsnano}, a Rust implementation of the Nano node~\cite{nano}. Account elections follow Section~\ref{sec:epoch}, with late notarization, the early-vote rule, and the prefix-wide overlap rule; checkpoints carry origin-stamped locks and are built by the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:close}. The equal-weight committee may have more members than $3f+2p+1$, which raises every threshold with it, and the fast path can be switched off. Epochs end at fixed times $T_0+kD$ on every validator's local clock. Every vote, frozen report, decided checkpoint, and overlap certificate is synced to disk before a signature that depends on it leaves the process, so a restarted validator refuses whatever its earlier signatures forbid. Set reconstruction uses rateless invertible Bloom lookup tables~\cite{rateless}. Agreement is one leader-based election per handover in the style of Kudzu~\cite{kudzu}: a validator votes for a proposal only after reconstructing its reports, running the recipe, and matching the state digest; leaders rotate, and a round whose leader stays silent times out after 2\,s. The supplement lists the prototype's departures from the protocol and the defects the measurements found.

\para{What we compare.}
\emph{RAI} is the protocol of this paper. \emph{Pause} is the same code with one switch flipped: no instance of epoch $e+1$ starts before $S_e$ is decided locally. It is the stop-and-drain transition RAI set out to avoid, with reports, recipe, agreement, and installation unchanged. \emph{Nano} is the RsNano \texttt{develop} node (commit \texttt{ff2b32710}) with one optimization of RAI's prototype ported to it, checking a fork block's signature once: a block is confirmed once final votes reach 67\% of the online weight, after a round of non-final votes. It has no epochs and no committee, and since final votes cannot be withdrawn, a fork whose votes split leaves neither side able to confirm.

\para{Setup.}
Six equal-weight validators run as separate processes on one MacBook Air (Apple M1, eight cores, 16\,GiB) over loopback. A load generator funds 48{,}000 accounts and then publishes 48{,}000 blocks at 2{,}000 blocks/s, for 24\,s, each on the frontier of a randomly chosen account, with the same random choices for the three systems. In the fork scenarios it publishes a conflicting sibling for 5\% or 10\% of the blocks, each sibling to half of the validators, and never builds on a forked position, so what is left open measures what the validators resolve on their own. In the Byzantine scenarios one validator runs a modified node: it votes for both blocks of every fork, signs two versions of its report and sends each to half of the validators, and leads the first round of every close with two different proposals. Nano's Byzantine validator votes for both blocks of every fork, the one attack Nano has a phase for. Unless stated otherwise RAI runs at Nano's quorum: $f=1$, $p=0$ on six members, so a certificate needs five of six, as Nano's 67\% does, and there is no fast path. RAI and pause use 8\,s epochs, so three closes start while the load runs. The \emph{confirmation rate} counts the non-fork blocks the client sees confirmed per second over the run. \emph{Latency} runs from a block's first publication to its confirmation at one validator and counts non-fork blocks only. After the load, the validators keep running until no block is open on any of them, or until none has become final for 45\,s. Each cell ran three times; we report the median and the range. At 2{,}000 blocks/s the host is close to saturation: the six validators compete for its eight cores, and in RAI an epoch close competes with voting.

\para{The same quorum as Nano (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-matrix}).}
Without forks Nano is the fastest: a median of 191\,ms and a 95th percentile of 237\,ms against RAI's 233 and 602\,ms, at 1{,}914 against 1{,}820 blocks/s; RAI's closes compete with voting for the host's cores. With forks the picture turns. Nano keeps its latency for the blocks it confirms, but with 10\% forks it confirms 557 blocks/s (479 to 953), while RAI confirms 1{,}500 with a median of 670\,ms; with 5\% forks the two confirm about as much, 1{,}792 and 1{,}695. Without a Byzantine validator, pause stays close to RAI: its median is within 10\% of RAI's without forks and with 5\% forks and 60\% higher with 10\%; RAI's 95th percentile is lower without forks and with 5\% forks, pause's with 10\% (3.2 against 4.3\,s). With one Byzantine validator, pause's median rises to 2.8 to 4.7\,s and its 95th percentile to 6.5 to 18.5\,s, while RAI's stay at 260 to 1{,}180\,ms and 0.7 to 3.3\,s; RAI confirms 12 to 52\% more blocks per second than pause, 1{,}514 to 1{,}812 against 996 to 1{,}616. Nano's Byzantine validator has nothing to attack without forks, and with 10\% forks Nano confirms 418 blocks/s.

\para{RAI resolves forks (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-forks}).}
After the load with 10\% forks, RAI and pause finalized every open block within 9.3 and 10.8\,s of the end of the load in all three runs, and every validator cemented the same state; with a Byzantine validator they took up to 16.2 and 35.8\,s. Nano never finalized them all. When it stopped making progress it had 245 to 1{,}258 blocks open with 5\% forks and 1 to 17 with 10\%, and with a Byzantine validator 78 to 321 and 603 to 649; in 8 of its 12 runs with forks its validators ended with different cemented states.

\para{RAI keeps confirming while an epoch closes (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-timeline}).}
With 10\% forks and a Byzantine validator, pause confirms nothing between the moment a validator leaves an epoch and the moment the checkpoint is decided, then confirms the held blocks in a burst: in the run shown it had eight seconds without a single confirmation during the load and averaged 503 blocks/s while an epoch closed. RAI averaged 1{,}525 blocks/s while an epoch closed. Its closes take as long as pause's, 4.7 to 9.6\,s against 3.6 to 18.4\,s in these runs, because the Byzantine leader's round must time out and every validator must reconstruct the version of the Byzantine report a proposal selects, but no block waits for them.

\para{A committee change (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-rotation}).}
With four seats ($f=1$, $p=0$), two members hand their weight to two standby validators in epoch~0, so the committee derived from epoch~0's state, the one epoch~2 runs, replaces both. RAI keeps confirming through the handover. Across the three scenarios it confirms 1{,}545 to 1{,}842 blocks/s with medians of 206 to 598\,ms, close to the six-member cells of Figure~\ref{fig:eval-matrix}, and resolves every fork. Pause's 95th percentile is 3.2 times RAI's without forks and 1.4 times with 5\% forks, and lower with 10\% (2.6 against 3.7\,s); Nano again leaves forks open (687 to 1{,}135 blocks with 5\%) and confirms 528 blocks/s with 10\%. With a Byzantine member that keeps its seat through the change, RAI's median is 233 to 1{,}003\,ms against pause's 1.7 to 2.2\,s and its 95th percentile 0.7 to 6.7\,s against 6.0 to 7.1\,s. RAI confirms more than pause without forks and with 5\% forks (1{,}821 and 1{,}606 against 1{,}776 and 1{,}483 blocks/s) and less with 10\% (1{,}122 against 1{,}240): with four seats every certificate needs all three correct members, and RAI's closes, which compete with voting, take 8\,s at the median against pause's 4\,s.

\para{What the fast path buys (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-fast}).}
With $f=p=1$ a certificate needs four of six and an uncontested block finalizes on one round of five settled first votes. Without forks this halves RAI's median, 111\,ms against 233\,ms, and lowers its 95th percentile from 602 to 341\,ms; with forks the median drops by 52\%, and by 23 to 40\% with a Byzantine validator. Both configurations tolerate one Byzantine validator; $f=p=1$ trades Nano's quorum for smaller certificates and the fast path. With 10\% forks and a Byzantine validator, where the host is saturated, the two configurations confirm about as much (1{,}411 against 1{,}514 blocks/s), and the $f=p=1$ cell, pooled over eight runs, varies from 884 to 1{,}524.

\para{Restarts and handoff.}
A second harness kills validators mid-load or as a close starts, restarts them on their data, and checks every logged signature for conflicts. Restarted validators rejoined by checkpoint catch-up, and a departing member killed when its last close starts and kept down 30\,s left every checkpoint decided identically. No run produced a conflicting signature. Synced signing costs about 130\,ms at the median and nothing in confirmation rate. The supplement reports these runs.

\para{Threats to validity.}
All validators share one host and a loopback network, so delays are dominated by CPU contention. At 2{,}000 blocks/s with 10\% forks every system confirms below the offered load, so those cells measure saturation rather than a steady state, and they vary most between runs. An epoch close competes with voting for the same cores, which is where RAI's latency tail comes from, and RAI does more work per block than pause (late notarizations and overlap evidence), so its confirmation rate may stay below Nano's on separate machines too. In one of nine runs with a committee change and 10\% forks, on an earlier build, closes fell further behind the epochs at each close and 704 forked positions were still open when the harness stopped waiting; the same seed resolved every fork when run again. Of ten runs with a committee change, a Byzantine member, and 10\% forks (the three of Figure~\ref{fig:eval-rotation} and seven comparing two builds), one left 321 positions open, identically on every validator, for the same reason: after the load each close outlasted the 8\,s epoch, and a contested position is first-voted only after its predecessor checkpoint is installed. Three runs per cell bound the spread only roughly, and process kills test crash consistency, not power loss.
