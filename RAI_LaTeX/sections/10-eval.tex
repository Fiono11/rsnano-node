\para{Prototype.}
We implemented RAI in RsNano~\cite{rsnano}, a Rust implementation of the Nano node~\cite{nano}. Account elections follow Section~\ref{sec:epoch} with the thresholds of Table~\ref{tab:thresholds} ($f=p=1$, $N=6$), late notarization, the early-vote rule, and the prefix-wide overlap rule; checkpoints carry origin-stamped locks and are built by the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:close}. Epochs end at fixed times $T_0+kD$ on every validator's local clock. Every first, final, late, and close-election vote, frozen report, decided checkpoint, and overlap certificate is written to its own LMDB environment and synced before a signature that depends on it leaves the process; a restarted validator reloads these records, refuses whatever they forbid, and fetches the checkpoints it missed. Set reconstruction uses rateless invertible Bloom lookup tables~\cite{rateless}: a validator that needs a report starts from its own view of the closing epoch and asks any holder for coded symbols until the symmetric difference peels, then checks the signed root. Agreement is one leader-based election per handover in the style of Kudzu~\cite{kudzu}: a validator votes for a proposal only after reconstructing its reports, checking the manifest, running the recipe, and matching the state digest, which gives eligibility; leaders rotate, and a round whose leader is silent times out after 2\,s. The supplement lists the prototype's departures from the protocol and the defects the measurements found.

\para{What we compare.}
Three systems run the same workload. \emph{RAI} is the protocol of this paper. \emph{Pause} is RAI with one switch flipped: no instance of epoch $e+1$ starts before $S_e$ is decided locally, so the overlap rule and late notarization never apply. It is the stop-and-drain transition that RAI set out to avoid, with everything else (reports, recipe, agreement, installation) identical. \emph{Nano} is the unmodified RsNano \texttt{develop} node (commit \texttt{ff2b32710}), which runs Nano's open representative voting: a block is confirmed once final votes reach 67\% of the online weight, after a round of non-final votes; it has no epochs, no checkpoints, and no committee, and since final votes cannot be withdrawn, a fork whose votes split leaves neither side able to confirm. All three are driven by the same load generator, built from the RAI branch.

\para{Setup.}
All validators run as separate processes on one MacBook Air (Apple M1, eight cores, 16\,GiB) over loopback, so there is no network delay and CPU contention sets the delays; a second series adds an emulated one-way delay of 40\,ms to every message every validator sends. Six equal-weight representatives hold the voting weight. A load generator funds 45{,}000 accounts and then publishes 45{,}000 blocks at an offered 2{,}000 blocks/s, each on the frontier of a randomly chosen account: it builds on a confirmed frontier, or extends an unconfirmed non-fork frontier with a fresh child once it has waited 5\,s, and it never builds on a position where it published a fork. With forks enabled it publishes a conflicting sibling for 5\% or 10\% of the blocks, each sibling to half of the validators. An \emph{offline} validator runs no node but keeps its weight; a \emph{Byzantine} validator also runs no node, and the generator votes with its key at random, choosing any vote kind for recent blocks and contradicting itself. RAI and pause use 8\,s epochs and sync every signing record before releasing it. We report non-fork blocks only: \emph{goodput} is the number of non-fork blocks confirmed divided by the time from first publication to last confirmation, and \emph{latency} runs from a block's first publication to its confirmation at one validator. A run \emph{settles} when every running validator holds the same cemented state and, with epochs, has decided the same checkpoints. Every variant ran three times per system, interleaved, and the figures show the median run with the spread of the three. The run records list three runs we excluded because the host slept during them or an operator error killed them, and their replacements.

\para{Without forks.}
All three systems keep up with the offered load, at 1{,}870 to 1{,}960 non-fork blocks/s (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-summary}). RAI's median latency, 116 to 150\,ms, is below Nano's 188 to 212\,ms (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-summary}): an uncontested block finalizes on one round of five settled first votes, where Nano always needs a round of final votes after its quorum of non-final votes. RAI's tail is longer than Nano's (95th percentile 254 to 566\,ms against 235 to 249\,ms); that is the cost of having checkpoints at all, since a block that lacks one of the four late supports waits for the checkpoint, and a close takes 4 to 7\,s. In one RAI run the client never saw one block confirmed although every validator had cemented it, so that run has no client numbers. The pause transition, with the same checkpoints, has a median like RAI's but a 95th percentile two to four times longer (703 to 1{,}168\,ms): every block published during a close waits for it.

\para{Over time.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-series} shows why, second by second, for one run per system and fork rate. Under pause, confirmations stop for two to three seconds at every boundary, and up to six at 10\% forks, until the checkpoint is decided, and the blocks confirmed right after carry the length of the close in their latency. Under RAI, confirmations continue through the close; they dip for one to two seconds at a boundary, while each validator freezes its report and carries thousands of open instances into the new epoch. Nano has no boundaries and confirms steadily.

\para{With forks.}
RAI and pause settled every fork run, with every running validator deciding the same checkpoints and cementing the same blocks, except one pause run at 10\% forks whose validators still disagreed on 347 blocks when the settle wait ended. Under pause, the client also never saw some non-fork blocks confirmed (0.6 to 4\% of them) in all three 10\%-fork runs with a validator offline and in two of three with a Byzantine validator; those runs have no client numbers. Nano settled every run except two of the three without faults at 10\% forks, whose validators still disagreed by a few blocks when the settle wait ended. Latency grows with the fork rate for both epoch-based systems, because forks make reports, manifests, and closes larger: RAI's median is 207 to 285\,ms and its 95th percentile 1.3 to 1.5\,s at 5\% forks, against 136 to 269\,ms and 1.5 to 3.7\,s for pause; at 10\% forks RAI's 95th percentile is 3.3 to 4.6\,s against 8.8\,s for pause. Nano's latency is unaffected at 5\% forks (95th percentile 238 to 242\,ms), but at 10\% its goodput falls to 536 to 582 blocks/s, a third of RAI's 1{,}280 to 1{,}630; we did not analyse why.

\para{Forks left unresolved.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-forks} shows how many forked positions had neither side final at the end. Because the generator never builds on a forked position, this measures what the validators resolve without the owner. These runs predate the termination rules of Section~\ref{sec:close}, which guarantee it. Nano resolved almost none of its 5\% forks (about 99\% left open) and half of its 10\% forks. RAI and pause resolved every fork in most runs. RAI left 1{,}491 of about 2{,}250 forks open in one of three 5\%-fork runs with a validator offline, about 2{,}900 of about 4{,}500 in two of three 10\%-fork runs with a validator offline, and 2{,}885 in one 10\%-fork run with a Byzantine validator. In each of these, two causes in the prototype combine. With a validator offline, a position split three against two looks to each validator as if it could still be notarized, so at the boundary every validator re-votes its old side and the split repeats in the next epoch; with all six online the split is recognised and the kept block is voted by everyone once the checkpoint is decided. And once the load stops, no new election starts an epoch's timer, so no further checkpoint is decided. The open positions are exactly those the last checkpoint kept, identically on every validator.

\para{Link delay (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-delay}).}
With 40\,ms added to every message, every close takes longer, and RAI's median rises to 251 to 303\,ms without forks and 431 to 487\,ms at 5\% forks. The rise comes from the closes, not from ordinary confirmations: outside a close RAI's median is 140 to 148\,ms, but its closes now take 4 to 9\,s of every 8\,s epoch, and the half of the blocks confirmed during a close need about 430\,ms, which is consistent with the second round that early votes require; we did not break latency down by finalization path. Pause keeps a lower median without forks (168 to 183\,ms) but its 95th percentile grows to 2.4 to 2.8\,s, three times RAI's 740 to 858\,ms; at 5\% forks pause is worse on both (median 689 to 984\,ms, 95th percentile 4.6 to 5.7\,s against 1.3 to 1.5\,s). Nano, which always takes two rounds, rises to a median of 272 to 274\,ms and a 95th percentile of 315 to 323\,ms without forks, and 277 to 285\,ms and 322 to 328\,ms at 5\% forks: RAI's median now matches Nano's, and outside closes it is about half of Nano's (140 to 148\,ms against 273\,ms), while its tail stays two to four times longer. The per-second view (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-delay}) shows the same pattern as on loopback: pause stops at every close, RAI keeps confirming at a higher latency during closes that take 4 to 9\,s, and Nano is flat.

\para{Restarts, committee changes, and handoff.}
A second harness kills validators mid-load or as a close starts, restarts them on their data, and checks every logged signature for conflicts. Restarted validators rejoined by checkpoint catch-up. With eight validators of which six form the committee, replacing two members, replacing them with a Byzantine member in every committee, and replacing every correct member of a four-member committee all settled with identical checkpoints everywhere, and a departing member killed when its last close starts and kept down 30\,s left every checkpoint decided identically. No run produced a conflicting signature. Synced signing costs about 130\,ms at the median and nothing in goodput. The supplement reports these runs in detail.

\para{Threats to validity.}
All validators share one host, so delays are CPU-bound even with emulated link delay; three runs per variant make the spreads indicative, not statistical; the Byzantine validator votes at random rather than following a script; and process kills test crash consistency, not power loss.
