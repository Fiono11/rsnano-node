\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\input{sections/eval-figures}
The evaluation asks three questions. Does RAI keep confirming payments while an epoch closes, where a stop-and-drain transition stops? What does it cost in latency against Nano, which has no epochs at all? And does it resolve the forks that Nano leaves open?

\para{Prototype.}
We implemented RAI in RsNano~\cite{rsnano}, a Rust implementation of the Nano node~\cite{nano}. Account elections follow Section~\ref{sec:epoch} with the thresholds of Table~\ref{tab:thresholds} ($f=p=1$, $N=6$), late notarization, the early-vote rule, and the prefix-wide overlap rule; checkpoints carry origin-stamped locks and are built by the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:close}. Epochs end at fixed times $T_0+kD$ on every validator's local clock. Every vote, frozen report, decided checkpoint, and overlap certificate is synced to disk before a signature that depends on it leaves the process, so a restarted validator refuses whatever its earlier signatures forbid. Set reconstruction uses rateless invertible Bloom lookup tables~\cite{rateless}. Agreement is one leader-based election per handover in the style of Kudzu~\cite{kudzu}: a validator votes for a proposal only after reconstructing its reports, running the recipe, and matching the state digest; leaders rotate, and a round whose leader is silent times out after 2\,s. The supplement lists the prototype's departures from the protocol and the defects the measurements found.

\para{What we compare.}
\emph{RAI} is the protocol of this paper. \emph{Pause} is the same code with one switch flipped: no instance of epoch $e+1$ starts before $S_e$ is decided locally. It is the stop-and-drain transition RAI set out to avoid, with reports, recipe, agreement, and installation unchanged. \emph{Nano} is the unmodified RsNano \texttt{develop} node (commit \texttt{ff2b32710}): a block is confirmed once final votes reach 67\% of the online weight, after a round of non-final votes. It has no epochs and no committee, and since final votes cannot be withdrawn, a fork whose votes split leaves neither side able to confirm.

\para{Setup.}
Six equal-weight validators run as separate processes on one MacBook Air (Apple M1, eight cores, 16\,GiB) over loopback. A load generator funds 45{,}000 accounts and then publishes 45{,}000 blocks at 1{,}000 blocks/s, each on the frontier of a randomly chosen account, with the same random choices for the three systems. In the fork scenarios it publishes a conflicting sibling for 5\% or 10\% of the blocks, each sibling to half of the validators, and never builds on a forked position, so what is left open measures what the validators resolve on their own. In the Byzantine scenario one validator leads the first round of every close and stays silent in it. RAI and pause use 8\,s epochs. \emph{Latency} runs from a block's first publication to its confirmation at one validator and counts non-fork blocks only. After the load, the validators keep running until no block is open on any of them, or until none has become final for 45\,s. Each scenario ran once. At 1{,}000 blocks/s none of the three systems is saturated; on this host all three sustain 2{,}000 blocks/s without forks and saturate between 2{,}000 and 3{,}000, where the six validators compete for the cores.

\para{RAI keeps confirming while an epoch closes (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-timeline}).}
Pause confirms nothing between the moment a validator leaves an epoch and the moment the checkpoint is decided, then confirms the held blocks in a burst. With 5\% forks its closes take 0.7 to 2.8\,s, and its confirmation rate falls to zero in three of them and to 70 blocks/s in a fourth; RAI's closes take as long, 0.9 to 3.1\,s, but its rate never falls below 616 blocks/s. Blocks published during a close have a 95th percentile of 1{,}638\,ms under pause and 381\,ms under RAI. A Byzantine leader makes every close wait for the 2\,s round timeout, so closes take 3.2 to 6.1\,s in both systems. Pause then confirms nothing in every close, 19 of the 44 seconds of load; RAI never confirms fewer than 323 blocks in a second, and blocks published during its closes, half of all blocks, have a 95th percentile of 394\,ms against pause's 4.6\,s.

\para{Latency (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-latency}).}
RAI has the lowest median in every scenario, 96 to 153\,ms against Nano's 175 to 210\,ms: an uncontested block finalizes on one round of five settled first votes, where Nano always needs a round of final votes. Its 95th percentile is 199 to 522\,ms; pause's is 2.9 to 13 times higher, 608\,ms without forks and 4.4\,s with a Byzantine leader, because every block published during a close waits for the checkpoint. Nano has no closes, so without forks or with 5\% forks its 95th percentile, 209 to 243\,ms, is close to RAI's. With 10\% forks it rises to 887\,ms and the 99th percentile to 83\,s: blocks behind a fork whose votes split wait for elections that never complete.

\para{RAI resolves forks (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-forks}).}
When the load with 10\% forks ended, 650 blocks were open under RAI and 678 under pause, forked positions and the blocks behind them; both finalized all of them within 12\,s, about one epoch and a close, and every validator cemented the same state. Nano still had 3{,}311 blocks open when its client stopped waiting 80\,s after the load, and stopped making progress with 8 to 81 open, a different number on each validator. With 5\% forks the picture is the same: RAI and pause finalized every open block within 9\,s of the end of the load, and within 15\,s with a Byzantine leader, while Nano left 617 and 448 open.

\para{Restarts, committee changes, and handoff.}
A second harness kills validators mid-load or as a close starts, restarts them on their data, and checks every logged signature for conflicts. Restarted validators rejoined by checkpoint catch-up. With eight validators of which six form the committee, replacing two members, replacing them with a Byzantine member in every committee, and replacing every correct member of a four-member committee all settled with identical checkpoints everywhere, and a departing member killed when its last close starts and kept down 30\,s left every checkpoint decided identically. No run produced a conflicting signature. Synced signing costs about 130\,ms at the median and nothing in goodput. The supplement reports these runs. A committee change in every epoch under load is beyond this host: with eight node processes, rotating runs completed at 500 blocks/s, but at 1{,}000 blocks/s and above a rotated-in validator fell behind its peers in most runs of both RAI and pause.

\para{Threats to validity.}
All validators share one host and a loopback network, so delays are dominated by CPU contention. At saturation RAI's close competes with voting for the same cores, and RAI does more work per block than pause (late notarizations and overlap evidence), so its peak throughput may stay at or below pause's on separate machines too. Each scenario ran once, so the numbers are indicative, not statistical. The Byzantine validator votes at random apart from leading every close, and process kills test crash consistency, not power loss.
