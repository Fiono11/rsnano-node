\para{Prototype.}
We implemented RAI in RsNano~\cite{rsnano}, a Rust implementation of the Nano node~\cite{nano}. Account elections follow Section~\ref{sec:epoch} with the thresholds of Table~\ref{tab:thresholds} ($f=p=1$, $N=6$), late notarization, the early-vote rule, and the prefix-wide overlap rule; checkpoints carry origin-stamped locks and are built by the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:close}. Epochs end at fixed times $T_0+kD$ on every validator's local clock. Every first, final, late, and close-election vote, frozen report, decided checkpoint, and overlap certificate is written to its own LMDB environment and synced before a signature that depends on it leaves the process; a restarted validator reloads these records, refuses whatever they forbid, and fetches the checkpoints it missed. Set reconstruction uses rateless invertible Bloom lookup tables~\cite{rateless}: a validator that needs a report starts from its own view of the closing epoch and asks any holder for coded symbols until the symmetric difference peels, then checks the signed root. Agreement is one leader-based election per handover in the style of Kudzu~\cite{kudzu}: a validator votes for a proposal only after reconstructing its reports, checking the manifest, running the recipe, and matching the state digest, which gives eligibility; leaders rotate, and a round whose leader is silent times out after 2\,s. The supplement lists the prototype's departures from the protocol and the defects the measurements found.

\para{What we compare.}
Three systems run the same code base and the same workload. \emph{RAI} is the protocol of this paper. \emph{Pause} is RAI with one switch flipped: no instance of epoch $e+1$ starts before $S_e$ is decided locally, so the overlap rule and late notarization never apply. It is the stop-and-drain transition that RAI set out to avoid, with everything else (reports, recipe, checkpoints, agreement) identical. \emph{Nano} is the unmodified RsNano \texttt{develop} node, which runs Nano's open representative voting: a block is confirmed once final votes reach 67\% of the online weight, after a round of non-final votes; it has no epochs, no checkpoints, and no committee, and since final votes cannot be withdrawn, a fork whose votes split leaves no side able to confirm.

\para{Setup.}
All validators run as separate processes on one MacBook Air (Apple M1, eight cores, 16\,GiB) over loopback, so network delay is zero and CPU contention sets the delays; a second series adds an emulated one-way link delay of 40\,ms to every message every validator sends. Six equal-weight representatives hold the voting weight. A load generator funds 45{,}000 accounts and then publishes 45{,}000 blocks at an offered 2{,}000 blocks/s, each on the frontier of a randomly chosen account, following the owner policy of Section~\ref{sec:close}: it builds on a confirmed frontier, or once an unconfirmed non-fork frontier has waited 5\,s it extends it with a fresh child, and it never extends a position where it published a fork. With forks enabled it publishes a conflicting sibling for 5\% or 10\% of the blocks, each sibling to half of the validators. An \emph{offline} validator runs no node but keeps its weight; a \emph{Byzantine} validator also runs no node, and the generator votes with its key at random, choosing any vote kind for recent blocks and contradicting itself. RAI and pause use 8\,s epochs. A run \emph{settles} when every running validator holds the same cemented state and, with epochs, has decided the same checkpoints; a position left kept by a split vote counts as settled when it is uncemented on every validator alike. We report non-fork blocks only: \emph{goodput} is the number of non-fork blocks confirmed divided by the time from first publication to last confirmation, and \emph{latency} runs from a block's publication to its confirmation at one validator. Every variant ran three times per system, interleaved, on an otherwise idle host, and we show the median run with the spread of the three.

\para{Without forks (Figures~\ref{fig:eval-latency} and~\ref{fig:eval-goodput}, left).}
All three systems keep up with the offered load. RAI's median latency is below Nano's: an uncontested block finalizes on one round of five settled first votes, where Nano needs a round of final votes after its quorum of non-final votes. RAI's tail is longer than Nano's, and this is the cost of having checkpoints at all: blocks that lack one of the four late supports wait for the checkpoint, and a close takes seconds. The pause transition shows what the overlap rules buy: with the same checkpoints and the same closes, every block published during a close waits, and the 95th percentile is several times RAI's.

\para{With forks.}
Nano finished none of the six fork variants: forks whose votes split stay unresolved and occupy election slots, delaying the non-fork blocks behind them; Figure~\ref{fig:eval-share} gives the share of blocks it confirmed before the publishing deadline. RAI and pause settled all of them, with every running validator deciding the same checkpoints and cementing the same blocks. Latency grows with the fork rate, since the retained positions make reports, manifests, and closes larger, and so does the gap between RAI and pause.

\para{Over time (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-series}).}
The per-second view of one 5\%-fork run per system makes the mechanism visible. Under pause, confirmations stop at every epoch boundary and resume when the checkpoint is decided, and the latency of the blocks confirmed right after a boundary is the length of the close. Under RAI, confirmations continue through the close, with a short dip at the install, and the close affects only the blocks that could not obtain their epoch-$e$ witness. Nano confirms steadily until forks accumulate.

\para{Link delay (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-delay}).}
With 40\,ms added to every message, Nano's two rounds and RAI's one round show in the medians, and closes take longer, which lengthens the pause transition's stalls proportionally.

\para{Restarts, committee changes, and handoff.}
A second harness kills validators mid-load or as a close starts, restarts them on their data, and checks every logged signature for conflicts. Restarted validators rejoined by checkpoint catch-up; with eight validators of which six form the committee, replacing two members, replacing them with a Byzantine member in every committee, and replacing every correct member of a four-member committee all settled with identical checkpoints everywhere; a departing member killed when its last close starts and kept down 30\,s left every checkpoint decided identically. No run produced a conflicting signature. Synced signing costs about 130\,ms at the median and nothing in goodput. The supplement reports these runs with their numbers.

\para{Threats to validity.}
One host, so the latencies are CPU-bound; three runs per variant, so the spreads are indicative, not statistical; a random rather than scripted Byzantine validator; and process kills rather than power loss.
