\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
A payment moves money from one account to another. If every account has a single owner, there is no need to put all payments of the world in one sequence: the owner orders the payments of their own account, and the validators only have to make sure that no two incompatible versions of that account's history both become final. Guerraoui et al.\ showed that this makes asset transfer strictly easier than consensus~\cite{consensusnumber}, and systems such as FastPay~\cite{fastpay}, Astro~\cite{astro}, Zef~\cite{zef}, and Nano~\cite{nano} use it to confirm a payment in one or two rounds of votes, with no leader and no block interval.

Such systems leave two problems open, and the two are connected.

\para{Changing the validators.}
Validators join, leave, and move weight. In Nano the voting weight of a validator is whatever the accounts delegating to it hold right now, read by every node from its own ledger, so two honest nodes can count the same vote with different weight and can disagree on who is in the quorum. FastPay fixes the set of validators for the lifetime of the system and lists reconfiguration as future work, because there is ``no natural point'' at which all authorities switch~\cite{fastpay}. The textbook remedy is to stop: end voting, wait until every pending confirmation has settled, agree on a snapshot, and start the new validators from it. Every transition then pauses the system for as long as the slowest confirmation takes.

\para{Accounts that contradict themselves.}
An owner who signs two conflicting payments for the same position of their account, by a wallet bug or on purpose, splits the validators' votes. If neither side reaches the quorum, no payment at that position can ever be confirmed. In FastPay such an account ``may become inaccessible''~\cite{fastpay}; in Nano a fork whose final votes split stays unresolved, and every later payment of that account is blocked behind it (we measure this in Section~\ref{sec:eval}). A double spend should cost the attacker, not the honest owner who made a mistake, and it should not make the rest of the system slower: the owner should be able to recover the account once they stop contradicting themselves.

\para{RAI.}
This paper presents RAI, a set of rules that gives an account-local payment system epochs, checkpoints, and committee changes without stopping, and that lets the owner of a forked account recover it unless a faulty validator backed both sides. Time is cut into epochs. In each epoch a fixed committee of validators votes on payments exactly as before. At the end of an epoch every outgoing validator freezes one signed \emph{report} of what it saw; the outgoing committee agrees on one \emph{checkpoint} built from these reports by a fixed recipe; and the next committee, known two epochs ahead, installs the checkpoint without voting on it. The checkpoint settles what the old epoch could still have confirmed and keeps at most one side of every unresolved fork, with a note of what may still happen to it. From then on at most one side can become final, and the owner recovers the account by building on the side the checkpoint kept.

The hard part is the overlap. RAI does not stop at an epoch boundary: validators start voting in epoch $e+1$ while epoch $e$ is still closing. Votes already given in epoch $e$ can still complete a confirmation after the boundary, and a checkpoint that ignored such a \emph{latent} confirmation could let the next epoch confirm a conflicting payment. Four rules keep the overlap safe (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}): new-epoch confirmations wait for the checkpoint unless they carry proof that the old epoch cannot contradict them; outgoing validators keep adding that proof for a short while; votes cast before their signer knows the checkpoint do not count toward the fast path; and a validator does not switch sides across the boundary. Three earlier versions of these rules looked right on paper and were wrong; TLA\textsuperscript{+} model checking found each failure in a few steps and verifies the repaired rules exhaustively for six validators (Section~\ref{sec:mc}).

\para{Contributions.}
(1)~A protocol for pipelined epoch transitions in account-local payment systems: immutable signed reports, a deterministic checkpoint recipe with \emph{locks} that record what may still become final and which epoch they protect, lagged committees, and the four overlap rules. (2)~A proof sketch of safety and termination relative to two abstract services, set reconstruction and agreement, whose interfaces RAI fixes but whose mechanics it leaves open; full proofs are in the supplement. (3)~Model checking of the handover rules, which exposed three failures of earlier rules and checks the repaired rules, including the rule that guards a whole account prefix. (4)~A prototype in the RsNano implementation of Nano, evaluated against unmodified Nano and against a stop-and-drain transition on nine fork and fault workloads, with repetitions, with and without emulated link delay, and under validator restarts, committee changes, and a crashing departing validator.

RAI is for authenticated committees with an externally maintained fault bound; it does not address permissionless membership, and its liveness promise to an owner who keeps contradicting themselves is only that their account cannot hurt anyone else.
