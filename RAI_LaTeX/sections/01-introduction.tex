\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Single-writer value transfer does not require every payment to occupy a position in a global transaction sequence. An account owner orders its own updates; validators only have to prevent incompatible owner-signed histories from becoming final. The consensus-number characterization of asset transfer formalizes this distinction, and payment systems already exploit it~\cite{consensusnumber,fastpay,astro}. The problem addressed here is what happens when the validators issuing those account-local votes are replaced.

A reconfiguration must decide which old outcomes the successor is obliged to preserve. Consider a payment for which enough outgoing validators have released signatures to form a final certificate, while network delay keeps those signatures apart, so that no checkpoint builder sees the certificate. If the checkpoint omits the payment and lets an incompatible successor history through, the delayed signatures later establish a valid old-epoch final outcome. The checkpoint was agreed, but it was not safe. We call such an outcome \emph{latent-final}, and we extend the obligation to certificates that outgoing validators which have not yet stopped may still complete.

The obvious remedy is to stop: end account voting, wait until every certificate has settled, then agree on a snapshot. That costs a pause at every epoch boundary, lasting as long as the slowest certificate. RAI avoids it. It is a protocol for \emph{pipelined} epoch transitions: epoch $e+1$ account voting starts at each validator's local boundary, while epoch $e$ is still closing, and the two are kept safe against each other by rules that are local to the account path. The closing side freezes one signed \emph{report} per outgoing validator, committing to a tagged view of its ledger and to the blocks it voted for outside that view; a deterministic construction turns a selection of reports into a checkpoint that preserves finality and carries obligation locks, and the successor committee, known two epochs ahead, installs it without voting on it.

RAI deliberately leaves two things abstract. Reports commit to sets by their roots; a \emph{convergence} service makes every correct reporter's frozen sets reconstructible at every correct validator. A \emph{consensus} service decides one checkpoint per epoch among the candidates RAI declares eligible. RAI specifies the interfaces and the properties it needs from both and proves safety and termination assuming theirs. Any set-reconciliation mechanism and any Byzantine agreement protocol with external validity can fill the two slots; our prototype uses rateless set reconciliation and a Kudzu-style election.

The pipeline is where the subtle failures are. Three rules of an earlier version of this protocol were wrong in ways that written arguments did not catch: a report format that hid a fresh vote for a block the predecessor had retained, a lock discharge that let two validators reach different verdicts on the same evidence depending on installation order, and a fast path that counted first votes cast before their signers knew the predecessor checkpoint. TLA\textsuperscript{+} models of the handoff exhibit each failure in a few steps and verify the repaired rules exhaustively for six validators (Section~\ref{sec:mc}).

\para{Contributions.}
First, RAI specifies pipelined epoch transitions for account-local finality: lagged committees, local boundaries, a predecessor gate for fresh successor work, a prefix-wide overlap exception that lets settlement continue before the predecessor checkpoint is known, late notarization by the outgoing committee, and a no-fast-path rule for early votes. Second, it defines immutable signed reports with a report-exposure invariant that covers latent and late finality, origin-stamped lock records with matching-origin discharge, and a deterministic checkpoint construction over eligible report selections. Third, it states the convergence and consensus interfaces as explicit assumptions, proves transfer safety and conditional termination relative to them, and model-checks the handoff rules. Fourth, it reports a prototype in RsNano whose nine workload variants, with forks and with one offline or one Byzantine validator, settle to identical state on every validator while the unmodified node finishes none of the forked ones, and it measures what the pipeline costs in latency tails.

The results are protocol arguments, checked by model checking only in the finite configurations of Section~\ref{sec:mc}. Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports single runs on one host; they illustrate behavior under load and do not validate the proofs' assumptions. The single-writer restriction and the two instantiated services are not claimed as contributions.
