\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\para{Prototype.}
We implemented RAI in RsNano~\cite{rsnano}, a Rust implementation of the Nano node~\cite{nano}. Account elections follow Section~\ref{sec:account} with the equal-weight thresholds of Table~\ref{tab:thresholds} ($f=p=1$, $N=6$) and the late notarization and prefix-wide overlap rule of Sections~\ref{sec:voting} and~\ref{sec:eligibility}. Epochs end at fixed times $T_0+kD$ on every validator's local clock. At its boundary a validator freezes and signs its report; the construction of Section~\ref{sec:build} includes Rule~3 promotion. Every first vote, final vote, late notarization, left epoch, and frozen report is written to an LMDB environment of its own before its signature leaves the process, one write per batch of votes the voter releases, and a restarted node reloads these records and refuses whatever they forbid; the environment is separate from the ledger's so that the voter never waits for the ledger's single writer. Late notarizations are generated apart from the consensus instantiation's votes, open no account instance at their receivers, and are kept apart from the vote records reports and manifests are built from. A position whose seen first votes are already split so that no block can reach $q$ is not re-voted at the boundary, since re-voting the same blocks would repeat the split. The two services of Section~\ref{sec:services} are instantiated as follows.

\para{Convergence by rateless set reconciliation.}
A report's two committed sets are reconstructed with rateless invertible Bloom lookup tables~\cite{iblt,whatsthedifference,rateless}. Each set element is a fixed 105-byte item naming account, height, hash, parent, and tag. A validator that needs a report starts from its own live projection of the closing epoch, which usually differs from the reporter's frozen set in a few entries, and asks for coded symbols of the frozen set; any validator that holds the reconstructed set answers, so the reporter is not a single point of service. Batches start at 16 symbols and grow to half of what has been received, up to 500. Peeling the symbols against the local projection yields the symmetric difference; the requester applies it and checks the signed root, which gives C1. In the runs below, reconstruction needed 1.5 to 2.9 coded symbols per differing item and no stream was abandoned. Before a report is usable, the validator fetches, by hash, the signed votes behind every $\tagN$ and $\tagF$ entry and every residual record it does not already hold, and rechecks only the entries that were missing. A report naming a block above the first position without its parent is malformed and never selected.

\para{Consensus by a Kudzu-style election.}
The closing committee decides the checkpoint with one leader-based voting instance that adapts Kudzu's voting construction~\cite{kudzu}: first votes, second-look notarization support, timeout votes, and certificates with thresholds $q$ (normal), $N-p$ (fast), and $q$ timeout votes to skip a slot, with Kudzu's finality-exclusion and prepared-window arguments unchanged. Election votes and account votes are separate domains, and every election signature binds the handoff context, the deciding committee, the slot, and the proposal hash or timeout value. Five points differ from Kudzu and are what makes the instance provide A1--A4. First, there is one instance per handoff, not a chain: slot zero is a genesis value, only a child of genesis may introduce a fresh payload $(Q,\mu_e,d_e)$, and a proposal on a certified non-genesis parent copies its parent's payload, so that every certified proposal in a decided history carries the same payload; with Kudzu's exclusion of a conflicting notarization or timeout certificate in a decided slot, this gives A1, and fixed-input construction gives the same state. Second, validity is RAI's eligibility: a validator first-votes a proposal only after reconstructing its reports, retrieving the manifest's votes, checking every claim against them, running $\Build$, and matching $d_e$, which is A2; a final quorum therefore contains correct validators that validated the selected reports and the fixed evidence, which with context binding gives A3. Third, a leader keeps the first fully usable report it holds from each identity; two correct leaders may validly select different reports of one equivocating Byzantine identity, and agreement, not an arrival-order rule, chooses between the candidates. Fourth, the outgoing committee decides and the successor committee only verifies the decision proof and reconstructs the state; it runs no second election. Fifth, Kudzu's erasure-coded payload distribution is not used and its communication bound is not claimed; reconstructing the inputs is a preparation step with its own cost. Leaders rotate through the committee in canonical key order; a leader proposes the candidate built from the canonical prefix of usable reports, so two correct leaders holding the same reports propose the same value and the first slot normally decides. The manifest digest $\mu_e$ covers, for every block a selected claim rests on, the committee members whose signed first and final votes the leader used, per epoch, as bit sets over the committee in key order. A validator whose own votes yield the same digest has checked that evidence already; otherwise it fetches the manifest by digest in chunks, fetches the votes it lacks, and checks every claim against the manifest's votes alone before voting. A slot whose leader is offline or Byzantine times out and the next leader proposes; under recurring prepared windows with $N-f$ responsive correct members and a local timeout satisfying $\Delta_E/(1+\rho)>\kappa_E+\tau_E+\delta$, where $\kappa_E$ bounds slot-entry skew and $\tau_E$ bounds proposal preparation and validation~\cite{dls}, a correct leader's valid proposal arrives before any correct timeout within one leader cycle, and Kudzu's progress argument gives A4. Successors install a decided checkpoint off the vote-processing path and fetch, by hash, any block of a retained or finalized branch they do not hold.

\para{Departures from the protocol.}
First, the manifest commits to signed votes; blocks and ancestry are placed by the report items themselves, and admission witnesses are not committed separately. Second, the consensus instantiation's own votes, the close election's first, final and timeout votes, are not persisted; durability of the account path and of reports is RAI's, durability of the agreement service is the instantiation's. Third, retained vote records keep a fixed cap of four epochs, since the reports of the next three epochs may still cite an epoch's late finality; only the handoff evidence, an epoch's frozen reports and their reconstructions, is released by acknowledgement, once members of the successor committee carrying $N-f$ of its weight have installed the checkpoint. Fourth, an inherited recovery lock is discharged by explicit compatible finality only; the matching-origin discharge witness of Section~\ref{sec:build} is not implemented, so a position whose votes split stays retained until its owner extends it. Fifth, the epoch origin $T_0$ is not persisted, so the harness cannot restart a validator within a run; the restart path is exercised by unit tests only.

\para{Baseline.}
The baseline is unmodified RsNano (\texttt{develop}), which runs Nano's open representative voting: a block is confirmed once final votes reach 67\% of the online weight, after a first round of non-final votes. It has no epochs and no checkpoints. Final votes cannot be withdrawn, so a fork whose votes split can leave no side able to reach the quorum; the baseline gives no termination guarantee in that case, and its unresolved elections keep their slots in a bounded election container.

\para{Setup.}
All validators run as separate processes on one MacBook Air (Apple M1, eight cores, 16\,GiB, macOS~14.6) and communicate over loopback; LMDB runs without synchronous writes, so the durable records of Section~\ref{sec:eval} survive a process crash but not a power loss. The election container holds up to 20{,}000 instances; RAI keeps every unresolved fork instance as evidence until its owner extends the position, and a 10\% fork workload leaves about 4{,}500 of them. Six equal-weight representatives hold the voting weight. A load generator funds 45{,}000 accounts and then publishes 45{,}000 blocks at an offered 2{,}000 blocks/s, each on the frontier of a randomly chosen account, so account chains grow during the run. The generator follows the owner policy of Section~\ref{sec:account}: it builds on a confirmed frontier, or, once an unconfirmed non-fork frontier has waited 5\,s, extends it with the account's next block as a fresh child; a position where it published a fork is never extended. With forks enabled, it publishes a conflicting sibling for 5\% or 10\% of the blocks, each sibling to half of the validators. An \emph{offline} validator runs no node and never votes but keeps its weight. A \emph{Byzantine} validator also runs no node; the generator votes with its key at random, choosing any vote kind for recently published blocks and contradicting itself. Both arms use the same load-generator source; the binaries differ only in the vote encoding, since RAI votes carry their epoch. RAI uses 8\,s epochs, so a run spans three checkpoints. A run \emph{settles} when every running validator holds the same cemented state and, for RAI, has decided the same checkpoints; a position left retained by a split vote counts as settled when it is uncemented on every validator alike. We report non-fork blocks only: goodput is the number of non-fork blocks confirmed divided by the time from the start of publishing to the last such confirmation, and latency runs from a block's publication to its confirmation at one validator. The baseline's publishing phase may last 1.5 times RAI's longest publishing phase for the same variant; setup time is not counted. Each variant ran once per system on an otherwise idle machine; the harness refuses to start while any other process uses more than a quarter of a core. The baseline rows were taken in an earlier pass on the same host with the same generator; the baseline code did not change. The RAI rows were taken in one pass on the build with late notarization.

\para{Without forks.}
Table~\ref{tab:eval} shows both systems keeping up with the offered load; RAI's goodput is 2 to 3\% higher. RAI's median latency is about half the baseline's, 104 to 110\,ms against 189 to 207\,ms: an uncontested block finalizes on one round of $N-p$ first votes, where the baseline needs a round of final votes after a quorum of non-final ones. RAI's tails are also lower, p95 176 to 305\,ms against 234 to 410\,ms. Closes took 1.2 to 2.7\,s when the first election round decided and up to 3.9\,s when a round timed out, but blocks published meanwhile did not wait for them: 91 to 96\% of the blocks late-notarized during a close reached a closing-epoch $\NC$ and finalized before the checkpoint, and 40 to 551 instances per boundary waited for the predecessor gate. Three checkpoints were decided on every running validator in all variants. The first two closes of every run decided in the first round; the third decided in the second round with one validator offline or Byzantine, when leader rotation reached the missing validator.

\para{With forks.}
The baseline finished none of the six fork variants: forks whose votes split stay unresolved and occupy election slots, which delays the non-fork blocks queued behind them, and the confirmed share at the deadline ranged from 69\% to 95\%. RAI settled all six with three checkpoints decided identically on every running validator. At 5\% forks its median stayed within 25\,ms of the no-fork median and p95 was 0.35 to 1.3\,s; at 10\% forks the median rose to 141 to 187\,ms, p95 to 0.8 to 2.1\,s, and goodput fell to 1{,}546 to 1{,}627 blocks/s. Forks lengthen the closes: more positions are retained at each freeze, reports and manifests grow with them, and a close took 2.2 to 8.6\,s at 10\% forks. They also leave more fresh blocks without a late $\NC$: 85 to 88\% of late-notarized blocks reached one at 5\% forks and 80 to 81\% at 10\%, the rest being split siblings and blocks one supporter short, which wait for the checkpoint; with one validator offline, the third close's second round adds the leader timeout to that wait. Most fork positions stayed retained: the generator sends each sibling to half of the validators, so a fork's first votes split three against three and neither side reaches $q=4$; without a notarization Rule~3 cannot promote either side, and the generator never extends a position where it equivocated. About 2{,}100 to 2{,}250 positions at 5\% forks and 4{,}360 to 4{,}480 at 10\% were uncemented when the runs settled, identically on every validator; they did not prevent settlement, and every non-fork block confirmed.

\para{What late notarization buys.}
The same workload was run the same day on the build without late notarization, the core rule alone. A block published during a close then has no closing-epoch $\NC$, so every such block waited for the checkpoint: 1{,}900 to 7{,}100 instances per boundary, client confirmations stopped for one to three seconds at each close, and p95 was 1.3 to 2.4\,s without forks and 2.1 to 8.2\,s with forks, with 10\%-fork goodput 15 to 25\% lower; medians were within 10 to 30\,ms of Table~\ref{tab:eval} except at 10\% forks, where the waits compounded. Late notarization keeps the core rule and its proof, costs one more signature per block and committee member during each close, and needs no members common to the two committees: its exclusion is within the closing epoch's own domain.

\para{Defects found by the matrix.}
Several defects were found and repaired while measuring; none is a failure of the two services. First, a bookkeeping slip in the report: a validator records, for every block it votes for, the parent that block names, so that its residual records place the block; the record was dropped when the slot state of the older of two consecutive epochs that both voted the block was discarded. The newer report then committed a residual record with a zero parent, $\Build$ failed at every leader that selected it, and two variants never closed an epoch. The record now outlives every slot state that votes the block, and a report naming a block without its parent is malformed and never selected, so one Byzantine report cannot stall the close the same way. Second, a block whose first votes split across an epoch boundary, three cast in the epoch left and two in the next, was retained as a recovery lock and never finalized: the validators that had voted it in the old epoch opened no instance in the new one, and nothing but an owner continuation resolves a recovery lock. Every unfinalized instance of the epoch left is now re-voted in the new epoch, which Section~\ref{sec:account} permits, and the generator implements the owner policy for the positions that remain. Third, manifest chunk requests were at first flooded to every representative; each returned a reply per chunk and the inbound queues overflowed, so two validators took four seconds to fetch an epoch's manifest and the round timed out. Requests now go to the proposal's sender. Fourth, re-voting every unfinalized instance doubled the permanently open fork instances and filled the election container to its former cap of 5{,}000, after which no new block was activated on any validator; a carried instance at a retained position whose lock cannot continue is now discarded when the checkpoint installs, a re-voted recovery lock continues once it holds an open-epoch $\NC$, and the cap was raised. Fifth, three costs of the handoff itself lengthened the wait at the gate: committee derivation deduplicated the epoch's finalized blocks quadratically, 0.2 to 0.5\,s per close on every validator before the gate opened; durable vote records shared the ledger's LMDB writer, so the voter waited behind block processing; and the gate was opened only after the committee bookkeeping. Sixth, late notarization first shared a vote generator with the consensus instantiation's notarization votes, which then queued behind thousands of late votes and slowed the close, and each late vote opened an instance of the closing epoch at its receivers, thousands of which were erased at installation.

\para{Limitations.}
Each variant ran once, so the table gives no run-to-run variance; in earlier repeated runs of this prototype, p95 varied by up to 40\% between identical runs, and the matrix was run seven times during the day, once stopped after three variants, with one variant failing to finish in two of them before the defects above were repaired. All validators share one host, so CPU contention rather than network latency sets the delays, and the fixed offered load measures neither saturation capacity nor wide-area behavior. The two arms were not interleaved in one pass, and the baseline rows predate the RAI rows by a day. The departures listed above mean these results validate neither the consensus instantiation's durability nor a manifest covering admission witnesses.

\section{Conclusion}
RAI makes epoch transitions for account-local payments pipelined and safe. The next epoch's voting starts at each validator's local boundary while the previous epoch closes; immutable reports expose enough of the outgoing committee's released votes to preserve latent and late finality; a deterministic construction over eligible report selections produces the checkpoint; and a predecessor gate with a narrow prefix-wide overlap exception, fed by the outgoing committee's late notarizations, keeps successor finality compatible with whatever the outgoing committee could still certify. The protocol defines the reports and the eligibility of checkpoint candidates and leaves reconstruction and agreement to two abstract services, convergence and consensus, with stated interfaces; safety and termination follow from their safety and termination together with explicit fault, data, and progress assumptions. A prototype instantiating them with rateless set reconciliation and a Kudzu-style election settles all nine workload variants, including forks with one offline or one Byzantine validator, keeping non-fork median latency near 100\,ms across three checkpoints; late notarization lets blocks published during a close finalize before the checkpoint, so that without forks the tails stay below those of the unmodified node, while under forks the blocks whose late notarization splits still wait for the checkpoint.
